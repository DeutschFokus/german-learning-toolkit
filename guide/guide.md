## Welcome — Read This First {#welcome}

**Willkommen!** You are about to start learning German — and this guide shows you how to do it efficiently, even with a busy life.

It answers one question: **what should you actually do, every day, to learn German from zero?**

The short answer:

::: steps
1. **Do a little German every day.** 20–30 minutes is enough. Even 10 minutes count.
2. **Speak from Day 1** — out loud, imperfectly, every day.
3. **Use a few good resources consistently** instead of many resources occasionally.
4. **Reuse your corrections.** A mistake you fix and reuse is worth more than ten new words.
:::

The long answer is the rest of this guide.

## The Levels at a Glance {#levels}

German courses, exams and this guide all use the **CEFR** — the *Common European Framework of Reference for Languages*. It describes six levels, from A1 to C2. This guide adds **A0**: your very first steps.

::: levels
| Level | Name | In real life, you can … |
|---|---|---|
| [A0]{.lv .blue} | Starter | say hello, your name, *bitte* and *danke* — and you know how German sounds |
| [A1]{.lv .blue} | Beginner | introduce yourself, order food, ask simple questions, understand slow and clear speech about familiar things |
| [A2]{.lv .blue} | Elementary | talk about your day, the past and your plans; handle routine situations like shopping, appointments and directions |
| [B1]{.lv .green} | Intermediate | handle most everyday situations, tell a story, give your opinion with reasons, follow the main points of clear standard German |
| [B2]{.lv .green} | Upper intermediate | follow normal native speech on most topics, discuss and defend an opinion, use German at work in many jobs |
| [C1]{.lv .purple} | Advanced | understand demanding texts and implicit meaning, speak fluently and spontaneously, use German flexibly for work and study |
| [C2]{.lv .purple} | Mastery | understand virtually everything and express fine shades of meaning precisely |
:::

### Three Zones

This guide groups the levels into three color zones. Each zone has a different main goal — and a different main speaking habit.

::: zones
| Zone | Levels | Main goal | Main speaking habit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 🔵 **Blue zone** — basic user | A0 · A1 · A2 | Survive and connect | One Natulang lesson every day |
| 🟢 **Green zone** — independent user | B1 · B2 | Express real ideas | Retelling, conversation, discussion |
| 🟣 **Purple zone** — proficient user | C1 · C2 | Precision, nuance and style | Debates, presentations, editing for style |
:::

## The Method — How to Learn German Efficiently {#method}

German does not require a perfect study system. It requires a good system that you actually use.

If you only have 20–30 minutes per day, your goal is not to squeeze ten different resources into your routine. Your goal is to make a few high-value activities automatic.

### The Four Pillars

Build your German around four pillars:

::: pillars
| Pillar | What it means | Core question |
|---|---|---|
| 🎧 **Input** | Listen to (and later read) German you can mostly understand | Can I understand more than last month? |
| 📖 **Course** | Follow one structured path — our lessons plus one course | Do I know what to learn next? |
| 🧠 **Vocabulary** | Remember useful words and phrases | Can I recall them when I need them? |
| 🗣️✍️ **Output** | Speak and write | Can I actually use what I know? |
:::

You do not need ten apps, six grammar books and four YouTube channels. A small number of activities produces most of your progress — these four, used consistently.

### The 20-Minute Rule

Twenty focused minutes every day usually beat one exhausted two-hour session at the weekend. Your memory is built by repetition and sleep — daily contact gives it both.

::: {.callout .tip}
**A simple 20–30-minute session**

- **5 min** — 🧠 review vocabulary (Anki or flashcards)
- **10–15 min** — 🗣️ speaking (from A0 to A2: your Natulang lesson)
- **5–10 min** — 🎧 listening, 📖 course work or ✍️ writing — rotate during the week
:::

**On busy days, do your minimum:** one Natulang lesson, *or* five minutes of flashcards plus three sentences said out loud. Even 10 minutes count. The goal is to protect your streak of meaningful contact with German — and if you miss a day, never miss two in a row.

### The Speaking Rule

Speak from Day 1.

Do not wait until your grammar is “good enough.” Say:

> **Ich heiße Maria.**\
> **Ich komme aus Spanien.**\
> **Ich wohne in Berlin.**

Simple sentences are real German. Imperfect German spoken today is worth more than perfect German that stays inside your head.

### The Natulang Rule

::: {.callout .natulang}
{{qr:natulang}}

**🗣️ From A0 to the end of A2: one Natulang lesson every day.**

Natulang is your daily speaking companion. A lesson takes **about 10–15 minutes**, and you answer everything out loud — the app listens and checks you.

This is your minimum daily speaking habit: a low-friction way to start speaking before you feel “ready.”
:::

**From B1 onward,** Natulang becomes optional. You can keep using it for review, pronunciation, automatic sentence building or extra speaking on busy days. But your main speaking practice should move toward retelling, spontaneous answers, conversations, explanations and discussions.

### The Article Rule

Every German noun has a gender: **der** (masculine), **die** (feminine) or **das** (neuter). The article changes other words in the sentence, so you need it from the very beginning — and it is far easier to learn it together with the word than to add it later.

> ❌ *Tisch* — table\
> ✅ **der Tisch, die Tische** — the table, the tables

- Learn every noun **with its article and its plural**.
- Say it out loud with the article: *der Tisch, der Tisch, der Tisch.*
- Color-code if it helps: many learners write **der** words in blue, **die** words in red and **das** words in green.

Some patterns make it easier:

| If the noun … | it is … | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| ends in **-ung, -heit, -keit, -schaft, -ion, -tät** | **die** | die Zeitung, die Freiheit, die Information |
| ends in **-chen** or **-lein** | **das** | das Mädchen, das Brötchen |
| is a day, a month or a season | **der** | der Montag, der Mai, der Winter |
| is plural | **die** | die Kinder, die Bücher |

### The Review Rule

Corrections are valuable only when you reuse them. Do not simply read

> ❌ *Ich warte dich.*

and move on. Write down the correct version:

> ✅ **Ich warte auf dich.**

Then make a new sentence with the same pattern:

> **Ich warte auf den Bus.**

Then use it again two days later.

::: big-goal
Notice → Understand → Rewrite → Reuse → Review
:::

### The 3-Pass Method

Use this for short videos, podcasts with transcripts, interviews and short texts.

::: steps
1. **Pass 1 — Support.** Listen or watch with subtitles in your strongest language if necessary. Understand the message.
2. **Pass 2 — German.** Use German subtitles or the German transcript. Notice vocabulary, structures, pronunciation and connectors.
3. **Pass 3 — No support.** Listen again without subtitles. Your brain now has enough context to hear much more.
:::

::: {.callout .tip}
**Important:** don’t use three passes for everything. Use the method selectively, for short and useful material.
:::

### Retelling — Your Main Tool From B1

Retelling turns passive understanding into active German. You will use it a lot from B1 on — and in a simple form already at A2.

**The 5-Keyword Method**

::: steps
1. 🎧 Watch or 📖 read something short.
2. Write down only **five keywords**.
3. Close the source.
4. Explain what happened in your own words — out loud.
5. Compare your version with the original.
:::

Example keywords: *Zug — Verspätung — Termin — Taxi — geschafft*

> *Gestern hatte mein Zug Verspätung. Ich hatte aber einen wichtigen Termin. Deshalb habe ich schließlich ein Taxi genommen. Zum Glück habe ich es noch rechtzeitig geschafft.*

This single exercise trains memory, vocabulary retrieval, grammar, sentence linking and speaking fluency.

## How to Get the Most Out of Our Lessons {#lessons}

Our lessons are where you get explanations, feedback and real conversation. But most of your progress happens **between** lessons — in your daily 20–30 minutes. This chapter connects the two.

::: {.callout .tip}
**📋 Before our very first lesson:** fill in **Your Learning Profile** — the short form that came with this guide — and send it back to me. It tells me your goals, your level and how you want to learn: book or conversation, how much you want to speak, homework, and how often we meet. About 10 minutes, mostly tick boxes.
:::

### Before Each Lesson — 10 Minutes

::: checklist
- Review your notes and corrections from the last lesson.
- Prepare **one or two questions** — about grammar, a word, or something you heard or read.
- Prepare **one thing to tell me in German**: what you did, something that happened, something you saw. Three sentences are perfect.
- Have your lesson notebook (or document) ready for corrections.
- If we meet online: test your microphone and headphones before the lesson starts.
:::

### During the Lesson

- **Speak as much as you can.** The lesson is your speaking time.
- **Manage the lesson in German** with the survival kit below — from the very first day.
- **Mistakes are welcome.** I will correct some, not all, so that the conversation can keep flowing.
- **Write down corrections and new phrases as complete sentences**, not as single words.

### After the Lesson — Within 24 Hours

::: checklist
- Review your corrections while they are still fresh.
- Put the **5–10 most useful** new phrases into Anki or your vocabulary notebook.
- Rewrite at least one corrected sentence — then make a new sentence with the same pattern.
- Do your homework, if there is any.
- Use one new phrase within two days: in real life, in a message, or out loud to yourself.
:::

### Your Lesson Notebook

Keep one place for everything from our lessons — a paper notebook, a document, or the [Lesson Notes](#t-lesson) template in Part 4. For every lesson, write down:

- the **date** and the main topic,
- **new phrases** — as complete sentences,
- **corrections**: ❌ what you said → ✅ the correct version,
- **questions** for next time,
- your **homework**.

### Your First 10 German Expressions

| German | English |
|---|---|
| **Hallo!** | Hello! |
| **Guten Tag!** | Good day! / Hello! (a little more formal) |
| **Tschüss!** | Bye! |
| **Ich heiße …** | My name is … |
| **Wie heißt du?** · **Wie heißen Sie?** | What’s your name? (informal · formal) |
| **Bitte.** | Please. / You’re welcome. |
| **Danke.** | Thank you. |
| **Ja.** | Yes. |
| **Nein.** | No. |
| **Entschuldigung.** | Excuse me. / Sorry. |

Say them out loud. Do not only read them.

::: {.callout .tip}
**du or Sie?** German has two words for “you.” Use **Sie** with strangers, officials, in shops and in formal situations. Use **du** with friends, family, children — and with anyone who offers it (*“Wir können uns duzen.”*). When in doubt, start with **Sie**.
:::

### Meet the German Alphabet

Pay special attention to the four letters English doesn’t have:

```{=html}
<div class="letters">
<div><b>ä</b><em>Mädchen</em><span>like the <i>e</i> in <i>bed</i></span></div>
<div><b>ö</b><em>schön</em><span>say “ay” as in <i>say</i> — then round your lips</span></div>
<div><b>ü</b><em>Tür</em><span>say “ee” as in <i>see</i> — then round your lips</span></div>
<div><b>ß</b><em>Straße</em><span>a sharp <i>s</i>, like <i>ss</i></span></div>
</div>
```

Don’t worry about mastering them immediately. Your first job is simply to notice that these sounds matter. All the rules are waiting in [Pronunciation & Prosody](#pronunciation) when you are ready.

### Your 5-Minute Starter Routine

::: steps
1. **Minute 1:** Say aloud: *Hallo. Ich heiße …*
2. **Minutes 2–3:** Listen to one very short piece of slow German.
3. **Minutes 4–5:** Repeat three familiar-looking German words — with their articles.
4. **Then:** complete today’s Natulang lesson.
:::

::: {.callout .natulang}
{{qr:natulang}}

**🗣️ Natulang — one lesson per day, about 10–15 minutes.**

Start today. Your job is not to sound perfect. Your job is to build the habit: **German goes into my ears → German comes out of my mouth.**
:::

### What NOT to Do Yet

::: dont
- Don’t memorize enormous grammar tables.
- Don’t study every possible article rule.
- Don’t translate every single word.
- Don’t collect hundreds of vocabulary cards.
- Don’t wait until you “know enough” to speak.
:::

Your first German should be small and usable.

At A1, you learn to handle simple everyday communication. Step by step, you become able to talk about yourself, your family, your day, your home and your routines.

### Your Four Pillars

::: pillars
| Pillar | Recommended | Backup | Minimum effective dose | Success check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 📖 **Course** | Our lessons + {{link:nicos-weg}} A1 | A structured A1 textbook | 15–20 min, 3× per week | I understand the grammar and vocabulary of my current unit. |

::: {.callout .natulang}
**🗣️ Your A1 speaking habit:** complete **one Natulang lesson every day.** This is your minimum speaking dose.
:::

If you have more time, add a short self-recording, questions in our lessons, a language partner, or talking to yourself while walking. But never replace daily speaking with passive study.

### What to Learn at A1

::: cols
- present tense of regular and common irregular verbs
- **sein** and **haben**
- word order: the verb in position 2
- W-questions and yes/no questions
- negation with **nicht** and **kein**
- nominative and basic accusative
- modal verbs: *können, müssen, wollen, möchten*
- separable verbs: *aufstehen, einkaufen, anrufen*
- possessives: *mein, dein, Ihr …*
- simple requests: *Komm! Kommen Sie!*
- numbers, dates and clock times
- common prepositions: *in, an, mit, zu, nach*
- your first past forms: *ich war, ich hatte, ich habe … gemacht*
:::

Do not try to understand every exception. Master the structures you need every day. For quick grammar summaries with exercises, see the free {{link:toolkit}}.

### High-Value Sentence Patterns

> **Ich komme aus …** — I come from …\
> **Ich wohne in …** — I live in …\
> **Ich arbeite als …** — I work as …\
> **Normalerweise …** — Usually …\
> **Am Wochenende …** — At the weekend …\
> **Ich möchte …** — I would like …\
> **Ich muss …** — I have to …\
> **Ich kann …** — I can …\
> **Ich mag …** — I like …

Watch the verb: ***Am Wochenende spiele ich Fußball.*** The verb stays in position 2 — so *ich* moves behind it.

### A1 Can-Do Checklist

::: {.checklist .cols}
- I can introduce myself in at least three sentences.
- I can say where I live and where I come from.
- I can talk briefly about my family.
- I can order something simple and ask the price.
- I can understand simple numbers, dates and times.
- I can describe a normal day.
- I can ask basic questions — and I know when to use *du* or *Sie*.
- I can understand very slow German about familiar topics.
- I can write a short, simple message.
- I can keep speaking despite making mistakes.
:::

### Weekly Speaking Test

Once a week, record yourself for one minute. Topic: ***Stell dich vor*** — introduce yourself. Include your name, country, city, job, languages and a hobby.

Don’t restart every time you make a mistake: fluency grows when you learn to continue. Keep your recordings — comparing this month’s with last month’s is the best motivation there is.

::: {.callout .pitfall}
**Common A1 pitfall: ❌ “I need more grammar before I can speak.”**

This often turns into months of preparation without real speaking.

**Fix:** do your Natulang lesson every day. Record one 1-minute self-introduction per week. Compare this month’s recording with last month’s.
:::

::: quote-box
🧠 **Your first 500 useful words unlock a new world.**

But 500 words you can **use** are worth more than 2,000 words you only recognize.
:::

### Grammar Priorities

#### 1. The Perfekt — talking about the past

> **Ich habe gestern gearbeitet.**\
> **Wir sind nach Berlin gefahren.**

Most verbs use *haben*. Verbs of movement or change — *gehen, fahren, kommen, aufstehen* — use *sein*.

#### 2. Two-way prepositions — Wohin? or Wo?

> **Ich gehe in die Küche.** — *Wohin?* Movement → Akkusativ\
> **Ich bin in der Küche.** — *Wo?* Location → Dativ

#### 3. The dative

> **Ich gebe dir das Buch.**\
> **Ich fahre mit dem Bus.**

The dative answers *Wem?* (to whom?) and always follows *aus, bei, mit, nach, seit, von, zu*.

#### 4. Your first subordinate clauses

> **Ich lerne Deutsch, weil ich in Deutschland arbeite.**\
> **Ich glaube, dass Deutsch interessant ist.**

Notice the verb: after *weil*, *dass* and *wenn* it goes to the **end**.

#### 5. Polite requests

> **Ich hätte gern einen Kaffee.**\
> **Könnten Sie mir bitte helfen?**\
> **Ich würde gern am Freitag kommen.**

Small words, big effect: these forms make you sound friendly and polite.

### 🧠 Learn Phrases, Not Bare Words

| Less useful | Better | Best |
|---|---|---|
| *erinnern* = remember | *sich an etwas erinnern* | **Ich kann mich nicht daran erinnern.** |

Useful chunks:

::: cols
- Angst vor etwas haben
- sich für etwas interessieren
- auf jemanden warten
- eine Entscheidung treffen
- Zeit verbringen
- einen Termin vereinbaren
:::

::: {.callout .natulang}
**🗣️ Your A2 speaking anchor:** keep doing **one Natulang lesson every day** until you reach the end of A2. Add a **5-minute German-only period** whenever you can.
:::

### Talk Around Missing Words

If you don’t know a word, explain around it. Forgot *Regenschirm* (umbrella)? Say:

> *Das Ding, das man benutzt, wenn es regnet.* — the thing you use when it rains.

That is real communication.

### ✍️ Weekly Writing

Write 80–120 words once a week. Possible topics:

::: cols
- What I did last weekend
- My next holiday
- A difficult day
- My favourite place
- A problem I solved
- My plans for next year
:::

Then **correct → rewrite → reuse.** Don’t just mark the mistakes.

### A2 Can-Do Checklist

::: {.checklist .cols}
- I can explain what I did yesterday.
- I can talk about my plans.
- I can make a simple appointment.
- I can explain a simple problem.
- I can ask follow-up questions.
- I can use *weil* and *dass*.
- I can use the Perfekt reliably.
- I can describe familiar places and people.
- I can manage a five-minute German-only conversation.
- I can write about 100 connected words.
:::

::: {.callout .pitfall}
**Common A2 pitfall: ❌ switching to English whenever German gets difficult.**

**Fix:** create a 5-Minute German-Only Rule. Once the timer starts, you continue in German — even if your sentences become very simple. Keep your daily Natulang lesson as your automatic speaking anchor.
:::

::: motto
Communication first. Elegance later.

Example keywords: *Wohnung — Nachbar — Musik — Nacht — Polizei*

### 🧠 Change the Way You Use Dictionaries

At B2, start using a monolingual dictionary such as Duden or DWDS alongside your bilingual one. Instead of only asking “What does this word mean in English?”, ask:

- Which words commonly appear with it? (DWDS’s *Wortprofil* shows you.)
- Is it formal or informal?
- Which preposition follows it?
- What is a natural example?
- Would a native speaker actually use it here?

### Build Vocabulary From Corrections

Your best vocabulary list is often not “50 new words from today’s article.” It is “10 expressions I tried to use — and used incorrectly.”

> ❌ *eine Entscheidung machen*\
> ✅ **eine Entscheidung treffen**

This correction closes a real gap in your German.

- Should university education be free?
- Is remote work better?
- Should social media be restricted for children?

> **Ein entscheidender Punkt ist …**\

### Your Goal

### Vocabulary: Stop Chasing More Words

At C1, your biggest gains often come from **precision**.

| Neutral | More precise alternatives |
|---|---|
| *wichtig* | wesentlich, entscheidend, maßgeblich, zentral |
| *deshalb* | daher, folglich, demzufolge, aus diesem Grund |

The question is no longer “Do I know another synonym?” It is: **which word fits this exact situation?**

### Register Training

Take one idea — *the company made a bad decision* — and say it three ways:

> **Informal:** *Die Firma hat da echt eine schlechte Entscheidung getroffen.*\
> **Neutral:** *Das Unternehmen hat eine problematische Entscheidung getroffen.*\
> **Formal:** *Die Entscheidung des Unternehmens erwies sich als strategisch problematisch.*

This is C1 work.

### 🗣️ Debate Complex Topics

Technological unemployment · social cohesion · privacy · education policy · the ethics of artificial intelligence · demographic change · environmental responsibility.

**Practise the full structure:** position → justification → evidence or example → counterargument → response → conclusion.

### ✍️ C1 Writing

Write 300–500 words. Focus on logical structure, paragraph transitions, precision, register, sentence variety and self-editing. Then run the [Big 5](#self-correction).

### C1 Can-Do Checklist

::: {.checklist .cols}
- I can speak spontaneously about abstract topics.
- I understand implied attitudes in many conversations.
- I can follow sophisticated documentaries and interviews.
- I can use formal and informal registers appropriately.
- I can summarize and evaluate someone else’s argument.
- I can reformulate an idea when my first wording fails.
- I can write clear texts of 300–500 words.
- I can identify recurring weaknesses in my own German.
- I can follow humour and some irony without everything being explained.
:::

::: {.callout .pitfall}
**Common C1 pitfall: ❌ “I feel like I’m not improving anymore.”**

At C1, progress becomes less visible. Going from “I don’t understand this” to “I understand this” is obvious. Going from “this sentence works” to “this sentence sounds more precise and natural” is much harder to notice.

**Fix:** track can-do statements, not feelings.
:::

::: motto
Advanced progress is often refinement, not expansion.
:::

## Pronunciation & Prosody {#pronunciation}

Good pronunciation does not mean erasing your accent or sounding exactly like one particular native speaker. The goal is:

::: big-goal
clear + effortless + easy to understand
:::

### The Essential Rules

#### Vowels

::: keep
| # | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Vowel length changes meaning.** Before a double consonant a vowel is usually short; a doubled vowel or a vowel + **h** is long. | *Stadt* (short) — *Staat* (long) · *Mitte* — *Miete* |
| 2 | **ie** is a long “ee”. | Liebe, sieben, spielen |
| 3 | **ei** sounds like English “eye”. *Trick: say the English name of the second letter — i**e** → “ee”, e**i** → “eye”.* | mein, klein, drei |
| 4 | **eu** and **äu** sound like “oy”. | heute, Leute, Häuser |
| 5 | **ä** sounds like the *e* in *bed*. | Mädchen, spät, Äpfel |
| 6 | **ö**: say “ay” (as in *say*) and round your lips. | schön, hören, Köln |
| 7 | **ü**: say “ee” (as in *see*) and round your lips. | Tür, müde, über |
| 8 | A final **-e** is always pronounced — a short, relaxed “uh”. | Name, bitte, Sonne |
| 9 | A final **-er** sounds like a short, dark “a”. | Lehrer, Vater, aber |
:::

#### Consonants

::: keep
| # | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | **s** before a vowel buzzes like English “z”; **ß** and **ss** are a sharp “s”. | Sonne, sieben, lesen · Straße, essen |
| 11 | **sch** = “sh”. | Schule, Tisch |
| 12 | **sp** and **st** at the start of a word or word part = “shp”, “sht”. | Sport, sprechen, Straße, verstehen |
| 13 | **z** and **tz** = “ts”. | Zeit, zehn, Katze |
| 14 | **w** = English “v”. | Wasser, wohnen |
| 15 | **v** = “f” in most German words — but “v” in many loanwords. | Vater, Vogel, viel · Video, Klavier |
| 16 | **j** = English “y”. | ja, jung, Jahr |
| 17 | **ch** after **i, e, ä, ö, ü, ei, eu** or a consonant: the soft *ich*-sound. | ich, Bücher, schlecht, Milch |
| 18 | **ch** after **a, o, u, au**: the rough *ach*-sound. | Bach, Buch, auch |
| 19 | A final **-ig** sounds like “-ich” in standard German. | wichtig, König, zwanzig |
| 20 | A final **b, d, g** sounds like **p, t, k**. | lieb, Rad, Tag, und |
| 21 | **qu** = “kv”. **pf**: pronounce both letters. | Quatsch, bequem · Apfel, Pferd |
| 22 | The German **r** comes from the back of the throat (or is rolled in some regions) — never use an English “r”. | rot, Reise, warum |
| 23 | **h** is pronounced at the start of a word, but silent after a vowel — where it makes the vowel long. | Haus, heute · sehr, fahren, Uhr |
:::

#### Stress and melody

::: keep
| # | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 24 | Stress is usually on the **first syllable of the word stem**. | ARbeiten, SPREchen, LEHrer |
| 25 | Separable prefixes are stressed; **be-, ge-, er-, ver-, zer-, ent-** never are. | AUFstehen, EINkaufen · verSTEhen, beZAHlen, entSCHEIden |
| 26 | **-tion** sounds like “tsion” and carries the stress. | InformaTION, StaTION |
:::

### Two Fossilized Errors to Prevent Early

**1. Vowel length.** Compare *bieten* and *bitten*, *Staat* and *Stadt*. One vowel is longer — and the difference changes the meaning. Listen before you guess pronunciation from spelling.

**2. Word stress.** Don’t pronounce German word by word with equal force. German has rhythm. Learn *eine EntSCHEIdung TREFfen* as one spoken unit.

### The Shadowing Technique

Shadowing means listening to a native (or high-quality) German recording and imitating it very closely: sounds, rhythm, stress, pauses and melody.

::: steps
1. **🎧 Listen.** Listen to one sentence several times. Don’t speak yet.
2. **🗣️ Imitate.** Repeat together with the speaker. Copy the music of the sentence, not only the words.
3. **🎙️ Record & compare.** Record yourself and compare stress, timing, vowel length, consonants and intonation.
:::

## Mediation — The Fifth Skill {#mediation}

Language is not only listening, speaking, reading and writing. There is another important skill:

::: big-goal
Mediation
:::

Mediation means helping information move from one person, language or context to another. You may summarize, explain, simplify, translate the meaning, clarify cultural context or bridge a misunderstanding. You don’t always need a literal translation — you need to preserve the message.

### Examples by Level

**A2 —** Read a short German text, then explain the main idea in your own simple words.

> **Der Zug fällt heute aus. Reisende können den Bus nehmen.**

Your explanation:

> *Der Zug fährt heute nicht. Man kann den Bus nehmen.*

**B1 / B2 —** Read a German news headline. Explain it to someone who doesn’t speak German: what happened, who is involved and why it matters.

**C1 / C2 —** Two people disagree because of different cultural expectations. Explain what each person means, why the misunderstanding happened and what nuance is missing.

### Practice Exercise

::: steps
1. 📖 Read one short German article.
2. Close it.
3. Identify the three most important points.
4. Explain them to a friend in your strongest language.
5. No friend available? Record yourself.
:::

::: motto
If you can explain information clearly, you probably understood it deeply.
:::

## Self-Correction & Editing {#self-correction}

Advanced learners improve faster when they learn to become their own first editor.

### The Big 5 — From B2 to C2

**1. Verb form & position.** Is the verb conjugated correctly? Is the finite verb in position 2? Does a verb belong at the end? Did I separate a separable verb correctly?

**2. Case.** Check nominative, accusative, dative and genitive — especially after prepositions, verbs and in adjective–noun combinations.

**3. Connector logic.** Don’t choose connectors because they “sound advanced.” Ask: *what relationship am I expressing?*

| Function | Examples |
|---|---|
| Cause | weil, da |
| Result | deshalb, daher, folglich |
| Contrast | obwohl, allerdings, dennoch |
| Addition | außerdem, darüber hinaus |

**4. Adjective endings.** Check article + adjective + noun:

> **ein interessantes Buch**\
> **mit einem interessanten Buch**

**5. Register.** Who am I speaking to? Is this formal, neutral, casual, academic or professional?

### Rewrite, Don’t Just Fix

> ❌ *Viele Leute benutzen Auto weil es ist einfacher.*\
> ✅ **Viele Menschen benutzen das Auto, weil es einfacher ist.**\
> ✅ **Viele Menschen entscheiden sich für das Auto, weil es im Alltag häufig praktischer ist.**

Correction repairs the sentence. Rewriting develops your German.

## Why This Works — The Science {#science}

You don’t need to become a neuroscientist to learn German efficiently. But four principles matter.

### 1. Spaced Repetition

Don’t review everything equally. Review information just before you are likely to forget it — for example on Day 1 → Day 2 → Day 4 → Day 8 → Day 16. Apps such as Anki automate this for you.

### 2. Interleaving

Don’t always study one grammar topic for an hour. Mix related skills: 5 minutes of vocabulary, 5 of listening, 5 of speaking, 5 of grammar. This forces your brain to decide which knowledge it needs — which is much closer to real communication.

### 3. Retrieval Practice

Re-reading feels easy. Remembering without looking is harder — and far more useful. Instead of re-reading *sich interessieren für*, cover the answer and ask: “Which preposition follows *sich interessieren*?” Then retrieve it: **für + Akkusativ.**

### 4. Desirable Difficulty

Don’t ask for the answer instantly. Struggle productively for a few seconds: try to remember, try to build the sentence — then check. That moment of effort is what makes it stick.

### Tool Audit

- Does this tool train a skill I actually need?
- Am I using it consistently?
- Can I explain what it contributes?
- Do I already have another tool doing the same job?
- Am I learning German — or organizing apps?
:::

If two tools do the same job, keep the better one.

## Realistic Timelines & Exams {#timelines}

Learning speed varies enormously — with the languages you already speak, your intensity, your teaching, your contact with German and your goals. The ranges below are for planning, not promises.

### How Long Does Each Level Take?

The Goethe-Institut estimates the following **total hours of learning from zero**. The two columns on the right turn those hours into months.

| To reach | Total hours from zero | Steady pace — about 4–5 hours/week (30 min a day + a weekly lesson) | Intensive pace — about 12 hours/week |
|---|---|---|---|
| [A1]{.lv .blue} | 60–150 | 3–8 months | 1–3 months |
| [A2]{.lv .blue} | 150–260 | 8–13 months | 3–5 months |
| [B1]{.lv .green} | 260–490 | 1–2 years | 5–9 months |
| [B2]{.lv .green} | 450–600 | 2–2½ years | 9–12 months |
| [C1]{.lv .purple} | 600–750 | 2½–3 years | 1–1¼ years |
| [C2]{.lv .purple} | 750+ | 3 years + | 1¼ years + |

::: source
Hours: Goethe-Institut, “Wie lange dauert es, Deutsch zu lernen?” (60-minute hours; 80–200 lessons of 45 minutes for A1, and so on). Months are calculated from those hours.
:::

**What counts as an hour?** Focused, active learning: lessons, course work, speaking, writing and attentive listening or reading. German playing in the background helps, but it counts much less. And the higher you go, the more you need real-life German on top of your study time.

### Exam Pathways

| Exam | Levels | Typical purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Goethe-Zertifikat** | A1–C2 | Internationally recognized general German certificates |
| **telc Deutsch** | A1–C2 | General, job-related and university versions (e.g. *telc Deutsch C1 Hochschule*) |
| **ÖSD** | A1–C2 | The Austrian German diploma, recognized internationally |
| **DTZ — Deutsch-Test für Zuwanderer** | A2–B1 (scaled) | The final exam of the German integration course |
| **TestDaF** | B2–C1 | University admission in Germany |
| **DSH** | about B2–C2 (DSH-1 to DSH-3) | University admission, taken at the university itself |

::: {.callout .tip}
**Which certificate do you need?** That depends on your situation, and the rules change. For example, an A1 certificate is often required for a family-reunion visa, and B1 is the usual requirement for permanent residence and naturalization in Germany. Always check the current official requirements for your case.
:::

### Exam Training Is Not the Same as Skipping a Level

You can sometimes prepare quickly for a particular exam format. That doesn’t mean the underlying language level has magically appeared. Short preparation periods usually focus on task types, timing, exam vocabulary, speaking formats, writing structure and scoring criteria. Free official model tests: {{link:goethe}}.

::: quote-box
**Exam technique can raise your score. It cannot replace the language itself.**
:::

## Bring German Into Your Life {#immersion}

You don’t need to live in Germany to create a German environment. Change small parts of the life you already have.

### Five Easy Immersion Habits

**📱 1. Change your phone language.** Switch some devices or apps to German. You will see words like *Einstellungen, Nachrichten, Speichern, Löschen, Weiter, Zurück* again and again.

**📺 2. Watch one German episode per week.** A series, reality TV, a documentary, an interview or an entertainment show. At lower levels, subtitles are fine. Your aim is not 100% comprehension — it is growing familiarity.

**🤝 3. Join real German-speaking environments.** Try a *Verein* (club), a *Sprachcafé*, a *Stammtisch*, a community event, a hobby group or a sports club. German becomes easier when it is attached to a real purpose.

**🎧 4. Do one daily task in German.** Read a recipe, read one news headline (try {{link:nachrichtenleicht}} from A2), listen to a five-minute podcast, check the weather in German, search for information in German.

**🗣️ 5. Use a language tandem.** Use a clear split — 50% German, 50% your partner’s target language — and don’t let the stronger language take over the whole conversation.

## Common Pitfalls & Fossilized Errors {#pitfalls}

| Trap | One-line fix |
|---|---|
| Pronouncing **ü** like **u**. | Isolate the sound and practise the mouth position deliberately: say “ee”, then round your lips. |
| Ignoring vowel length. | Copy whole words from audio instead of trusting the spelling. |
| Learning nouns without articles. | Learn **der / die / das + plural** with every noun, from Day 1. |
| Avoiding speaking. | Speak every day, even for five minutes. |
| Looking up every unknown word. | Ask whether the word blocks understanding. If not, keep going. |
| App-hopping. | Keep one main tool per learning job. |
| Collecting vocabulary without reviewing it. | Retrieval beats collection. |
| Ignoring corrections. | Put recurring corrections into your mistake log. |
| Only consuming easy material. | Keep roughly 10–20% of your study challenging. |

### Special A0–A2 Warning

The most damaging beginner trap is:

::: big-goal
“I’m not ready to speak yet.”
:::

You become ready to speak **by speaking.**

::: {.callout .natulang}
**Fix:** complete **one Natulang lesson every day** from A0 through the end of A2. Five minutes of imperfect speaking beat zero minutes of perfect intentions.
:::

## Motivation & Mindset {#mindset}

Motivation changes. Your system has to survive the days when motivation disappears.

### The Intermediate Plateau

At beginner level, progress is dramatic. At B1–C1, the changes get smaller: you move from “I can explain this” to “I can explain this clearly, naturally and with better vocabulary.” That improvement is real — but harder to feel. **Track evidence, not emotion.**

### The Good-Mistake Mindset

A useful mistake tells you: *here is the edge of my current ability.* That is valuable information. If you never make mistakes, your practice is probably too easy.

### The Bad-Day Rule

Never judge your whole German on one bad conversation. You may be tired, stressed or distracted, talking about an unfamiliar subject, or talking to a difficult speaker. Language ability fluctuates. Long-term performance is what counts.

### Never Miss Twice

Missing one day is normal. Missing two in a row starts a new habit — so after a missed day, do at least your minimum the next day.

### Celebrate Small Wins

::: {.checklist .cols}
- My first full German conversation
- My first phone call in German
- My first joke understood
- My first podcast understood without subtitles
- My first page read without a dictionary
- My first compliment on my German
- My first spontaneous dream in German
- My first time explaining something complicated
:::

### When You Feel Stuck

| Problem | Redirect |
|---|---|
| Am I getting enough input? | Increase listening and reading. |
| Do I understand, but freeze when speaking? | Increase retelling and spontaneous output. |
| Do I speak fluently but inaccurately? | Increase correction and deliberate editing. |
| Do I forget vocabulary? | Increase retrieval and spaced repetition. |
| Am I overwhelmed? | Reduce the number of resources. |
| Am I bored? | Increase the difficulty or change the topic — not the whole system. |
